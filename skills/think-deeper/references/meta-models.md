# Meta Mental Models Reference

Source: Dr. Justin Sung's 6 Meta Mental Models framework.

These are not thinking tools in the traditional sense (like first principles or inversion). They are meta-cognitive checks -- patterns that catch the ways your brain shortcuts around hard thinking.

---

## 1. Non-Linearity Check

**Definition:** Most real-world systems are non-linear. Variables interact, amplify, and dampen each other in ways that simple cause-and-effect chains miss. This model forces you to map the actual web of relationships instead of assuming A leads to B.

**What it catches:** Linear thinking. The instinct to draw a straight line from problem to solution without accounting for feedback loops, second-order effects, or confounding variables.

**Red flags:**
- "If we just do X, then Y will happen"
- Ignoring variables that don't fit the narrative
- Single-cause explanations for multi-factor outcomes
- Plans with no contingencies

**Challenge question:** "You described [X] as leading to [Y]. What other variables could influence that relationship?"

**Example:** A founder says "if we lower prices, we'll get more customers." Non-linearity check: lower prices may signal lower quality, attract price-sensitive customers with higher churn, compress margins so you can't invest in support, and create a race to the bottom with competitors. The relationship between price and customer acquisition is not a straight line.

---

## 2. Gray Thinking Check

**Definition:** Binary framing feels decisive but often eliminates the best options. Most decisions exist on a spectrum. Gray thinking finds the territory between the poles.

**What it catches:** False dichotomies. Either/or framing that makes you choose between two extremes when a hybrid, phased, or conditional approach would be stronger.

**Red flags:**
- "We either do X or Y"
- "It's this or nothing"
- Presenting only two options when asked for solutions
- Framing trade-offs as zero-sum

**Challenge question:** "You said [X] vs [Y]. What does a solution look like that combines elements of both?"

**Example:** "We either build it in-house or outsource it." Gray thinking: build the core in-house and outsource the commodity layers. Or prototype in-house, validate the concept, then bring in contractors to scale the implementation. The spectrum between build and buy has dozens of viable positions.

---

## 3. Occam's Bias Check

**Definition:** Occam's Razor says the simplest explanation is usually correct. That's often true. But your brain weaponizes this principle to avoid doing hard analysis. The question is whether you're simplifying because you've earned the right to (you've looked at the complexity and triaged it) or because you haven't bothered to look.

**What it catches:** Over-attribution. Lazy simplification disguised as elegant thinking. Lumping multiple distinct problems under one label because it's easier to deal with.

**Red flags:**
- Attributing multiple symptoms to a single root cause without evidence
- "The real problem is just X"
- Dismissing complexity as overthinking
- Resisting when someone adds nuance to your model

**Key distinction:** Intentional simplification (you've examined the complexity and chosen what to ignore, knowing the cost) vs. lazy simplification (you haven't looked and you're pattern-matching to avoid the work).

**Challenge question:** "You've attributed [multiple symptoms] to [single cause]. Could there be multiple contributing factors?"

**Example:** "Our churn is high because the product is too complicated." Occam's bias check: is it the product? Or is it onboarding? Or is it that the sales team is selling to the wrong ICP? Or that your pricing attracts experimenters who were never going to stick? Each of these is a different problem with a different fix.

---

## 4. Framing Bias Check

**Definition:** The way a problem is presented to you determines which solutions you consider. If you accept the default frame without questioning it, you're solving someone else's version of the problem.

**What it catches:** Anchoring to the first framing you received. Solving the stated problem instead of the actual problem. Letting the messenger's perspective become your perspective.

**Red flags:**
- Using the exact language the problem was presented in
- Never asking "but is that the real problem?"
- Solutions that only address the stated symptom
- Accepting constraints without testing whether they're real

**Challenge question:** "If you had to explain this problem to someone in a totally different industry, how would you describe it?"

**Example:** "We need a better dashboard." Reframe: do you need a better dashboard, or do you need people to make better decisions? Maybe the problem isn't the dashboard -- maybe it's that people don't know what metrics matter, or they're drowning in data, or the decisions are being made in Slack threads and the dashboard is irrelevant.

---

## 5. Anti-Comfort Check

**Definition:** Your brain defaults to patterns that worked before. Past success creates confidence that feels like insight but is actually just familiarity. This model forces you to test whether your thinking is validated or just comfortable.

**What it catches:** Over-confidence from past experience. Applying old playbooks to new situations. Confirmation bias dressed up as pattern recognition.

**Red flags:**
- "We did this before and it worked"
- High confidence with low evidence
- Dismissing counter-arguments quickly
- Gravitating toward the familiar approach without evaluating alternatives

**Challenge question:** "If your biggest competitor was solving this same problem, what would they do differently?"

**Example:** A marketing team keeps running the same campaign format because "it's always worked." Anti-comfort check: the market has changed, the audience has changed, the channels have changed. What worked in 2023 might be leaving 80% of the opportunity on the table in 2026. The comfort of "proven" prevents testing what could be 10x better.

---

## 6. Delayed Discomfort Check

**Definition:** The easiest option now almost always has a hidden cost that shows up later. This model forces you to project forward and price the future consequences of today's easy path.

**What it catches:** Kicking the can. Accumulating decision debt, technical debt, or relationship debt by choosing comfort now over correctness now.

**Red flags:**
- "We'll deal with that later"
- "This is fine for now"
- Choosing the faster option without pricing the rework
- Avoiding a hard conversation today that will be harder tomorrow

**Challenge question:** "What problem are you creating for future-you by choosing the simpler option today?"

**Example:** "Let's just hardcode the API key for now and fix it later." Delayed discomfort check: "later" has a compounding cost. Every week that key sits in the codebase, more code depends on it, more developers copy the pattern, and the blast radius of the eventual breach grows. The 30 minutes you save today could cost 30 hours (or a security incident) in 6 months.
